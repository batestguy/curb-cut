Title card (0:00.0)

Curb Cut: Nigerian disability data, measured

Act 1: Hook (0:02.8)

How many disabled people live in Nigeria? You often hear thirty-five million. That's a figure from the Joint National Association of People with Disabilities in 2025, which itself is an estimate from the global prevalence of fifteen percent of the population in Nigeria. That global rate was found in a study by the World Bank and the World Health Organization in 2011. My name is Jerry Bannister. I'm a statistician in Bauchi and a wheelchair user. Come along with me to see what the surveys actually measured.

On-screen caption: JONAPWD 2025: 232,676,020 (NPC 2025 projection) × 15% (WHO and World Bank global estimate) = 34,901,403: assumed, not counted (on screen from 0:07.6)

Act 2: The measured answer (0:31.9)

Nigeria's 2024 Demographic and Health Survey asked the Washington Group questions. That's the international standard. Three in a hundred adults have a lot of difficulty or completely lack the ability to engage in seeing, hearing, walking, remembering, caring for themselves or communicating. The official report, counting those with any difficulty at all, gets about fifteen in a hundred people that are aged fifteen and over. But that's a different question. The report already publishes state figures for men and women separately, without any margins of error. I combined them into one figure per state, which roughly doubles each state's sample, and gave every state a confidence interval using the survey data and sampling weights.

On-screen caption: Nigeria DHS 2024 · adults 18+: 3.0% (95% CI 2.8–3.2) (on screen from 0:38.8)

On-screen caption: NDHS report, ages 15+: any difficulty 15.5% · at the Washington Group cut-off 2.8% (on screen from 0:49.3)

On-screen caption: R survey, checked in Python svy (5,816 cells) · 1,360 official report cells reproduced (on screen from 1:05.7)

Act 3: What was found, and what wasn't (1:15.4)

The states are not alike. In Taraba, it's about eleven in a hundred. In Lagos, it's under one in a hundred. Seven states are clearly above the national figure, and twelve clearly below after correcting for multiple testing. But two things I expected didn't hold. Neighbouring states don't show a clear pattern of being alike. A spatial test was conducted, Moran's I, and it found none of the claims survives correction. As a follow-up, a small-area model meant to sharpen the state figures failed the checks I set in advance [correction: the small-area model failed the checks set in advance], so I also left it out.

On-screen caption: Taraba 11.3% · Lagos 0.8% · 7 above, 12 below national (Benjamini-Hochberg, FDR 5%) (on screen from 1:17.6)

On-screen caption: No geographic pattern survives correction: Moran's I 0.13, adjusted p 0.105 (37 states, low power; not proof of no pattern) (on screen from 1:31.2)

On-screen caption: Fay-Herriot small-area model failed its pre-declared diagnostics: not used (on screen from 1:42.0)

On-screen correction: the small-area model failed the checks set in advance. (footnote on screen 1:44.8–1:49.8)

Act 4: Poverty (1:49.8)

So, does disability go with poverty? I registered the plan publicly before I split poverty by disability. Our results were expected. Using double machine learning on the national poverty survey, people in households with a member who has severe difficulty are about twelve percentage points more likely to be poor on the national multidimensional poverty index than people in comparable households. The range is six to seventeen percent [correction: 6 to 17 percentage points (95% CI 6.1–17.2)]. When I shuffled who was disabled a hundred times, the fake results averaged almost about zero. But I'll be honest about the weak spots. In the worst case, a hidden factor I didn't measure, even a small one, could explain it away. A factor as strong as the head of household's education would barely move it, but it would be impossible to rule out something stronger. So it's an association, and not proof of cause. A smaller panel survey looks at work. Among people whose severe difficulty began between 2018 and 2023, the proportion working fell about twenty-six points more than among comparable people whose difficulty didn't begin during that time period. That part is exploratory, and it can't say which came first.

On-screen caption: NBS MPI 2021 · pre-registered at osf.io/kes3p · DoubleML: +11.7 points (95% CI 6.1–17.2) (on screen from 1:59.6)

On-screen caption: 100 placebo shuffles: mean 0.6 points, empirical p = 0.010 (on screen from 2:15.7)

On-screen caption: Fragile to hidden confounding: robustness value 1.8% · association, not cause (on screen from 2:24.3)

On-screen caption: GHS-Panel 2018/19 → 2023/24, exploratory: working −26.4 points relative to comparable people without onset (−33.2 to −19.7); consumption: survey cannot show a difference (on screen from 2:41.3)

On-screen correction: 6 to 17 percentage points (95% CI 6.1–17.2). (footnote on screen 2:11.9–2:16.9)

Act 4b: What wasn't significant (2:59.7)

A lot of what I tested wasn't significant, and the site says so. The state chart tested seventy-three comparisons: neighbouring states and each state were tested against the national figure [correction: the 73 comparisons are states next to each other in the ranking, the highest and lowest, and each state against the national figure]. Only about twenty held up after corrections for multiple testing. Taraba, being the highest, can't [this word is unclear in the recording; the on-screen footnote states the finding] be told apart from Abia, the second highest. Looking at where the poverty difference sits, the deprivation gap is clear for health, for work and shocks, but the survey can't show one for education or living standards. The panel survey also fails to show a difference in how household consumption changed. As a check, the site can't deploy unless every page and its types score a hundred on the Lighthouse accessibility checks.

On-screen caption: State chart: 73 comparisons tested, 20 significant (Benjamini-Hochberg) · Taraba vs Abia: no detectable difference (on screen from 3:04.5)

On-screen caption: Not significant: education and living-standards deprivation differences · GHS consumption: survey cannot show a difference (on screen from 3:20.8)

On-screen caption: Deploy gate: Lighthouse accessibility 100 on every page type (on screen from 3:36.3)

On-screen correction: the 73 comparisons are states next to each other in the ranking, the highest and lowest, and each state against the national figure. (footnote on screen 3:07.1–3:14.8)

On-screen correction: Taraba can't be told apart from Abia: their intervals overlap. (footnote on screen 3:15.1–3:20.1)

Act 5b: My library, in action (4:42.0)

This is my open-source Python library. It's called disability-access-charts. It builds each chart with a description written from the data, and then checks that text against the data, with intervals and all. Let's take a look. The check: “Taraba has a higher rate than Lagos” passes, but “Taraba has a higher rate than Abia” is rejected. The gap is small, and the uncertainty doesn't allow it. The site's descriptions are built only from the differences that held up, and this check runs in strict mode on every chart before the site is built, as a second guard.

On-screen caption: pip install disability-access-charts · open source (MIT) (on screen from 4:42.3)

On-screen caption: check_alt_text(): "Taraba > Lagos" passes · "Taraba > Abia" rejected (difference 0.70, needs more than 3.74) (on screen from 4:57.2)

On-screen caption: CI: disability-access-charts check --strict on every chart before the site is built (on screen from 5:11.8)

Every chart describes itself, and my open-source library checks the numbers and comparisons in those words against the data before the site is built. It's free, every figure names its source, and it hasn't yet been tested by a regular screen-reader user. If you use one, tell me what works and what doesn't. Thank you.

Note on corrections and transcription

The author chose on-screen correction footnotes over re-recording. Four footnotes are shown on screen: in act 3, the small-area model failed the checks set in advance (the recording may sound like "filled"); in act 4, the range is 6 to 17 percentage points, not percent (95% CI 6.1–17.2); in act 4b, the 73 comparisons are states next to each other in the ranking, the highest and lowest, and each state against the national figure (said as "neighbouring states and each state were tested against the national figure"). Also in act 4b, whether the author says "can" or "can't" before "be told apart from Abia" is unclear in the recording; the footnote states the finding (Taraba can't be told apart from Abia). Act 1's opening differs from the script by the author's choice; it is not corrected.

Speech-recognition errors fixed in this transcript (what the recogniser heard → what was said): Gerry → Jerry; survey is → surveys; Maran's eye → Moran's I; special test → spatial test; shoveled → shuffled; proof of course → proof of cause; Smaller panels of A → A smaller panel survey; which game first → which came first; them in three → seventy-three; Tarabah → Taraba; The panels will be also filled to show → The panel survey also fails to show; every pitch and its types cause a hundred → every page and its types score a hundred; The checked error bar has a higher read than Legos → The check: “Taraba has a higher rate than Lagos”; error bar has a higher read than RBIs → “Taraba has a higher rate than Abia”; side descriptions → site's descriptions; the difference that I held up → the differences that held up; strict mood → strict mode; every forgot name is its source → every figure names its source. Spoken numbers are written in words, as in the script.
